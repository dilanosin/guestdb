Where they call home

Two islands, one remarkable mix of habitats — and every one has its snakes.

  • ✓Northern Range — wet montane forest; look for boas, the bushmaster and tree boas.
  • ✓Cocoa & coffee estates — silky snakes and fer-de-lances hunting rats on the ridges.
  • ✓Lowland & savanna — macajuels and garden snakes around villages and fields.
  • ✓Tobago's forests — quieter, drier; a handful of the same familiar species.

Stay safe

Living beside snakes, safely

A few simple habits keep you, your family and the snakes safe.

🛡️

Do not kill

All snakes are protected by the Conservation of Wildlife Act in T&T. Killing one is against the law.

⚠️

Respect the Mapepire

Back away slowly and give it space. It will always move off on its own — attacks are almost always provoked.

📞

Call for relocation

For a snake in your yard, contact the Wildlife Section or an experienced snake relocator — never handle one yourself.

🚑 If someone is bitten

Keep the person calm and still. Do not cut, suck or apply a tight tourniquet. Get to a hospital immediately.

Snakebite protocol

First aid for a snakebite

Time matters. This protocol follows current WHO guidance on snakebite first aid for the Caribbean. It is a first-aid guide — treat it as the first step, never as a substitute for hospital care.

Protocol

Immediate actions — in order

Carry out these steps before and during evacuation. The goal is to slow venom spread and get the patient to definitive care as quickly and safely as possible.

  1. 01
    Move the patient out of striking range

    Keep at least 5 m from the snake. Do not attempt to capture, kill or identify it in person — an accurate verbal description from a safe distance is enough.

  2. 02
    Activate emergency medical services

    Call 811 (ambulance) or 999 (police/emergency) immediately. State that a snakebite occurred, give the location, and ask to be routed to the nearest facility holding antivenom.

  3. 03
    Keep the patient calm and at rest

    Reassure them; anxiety and movement raise heart rate and accelerate venom distribution. Have the patient lie supine with the bitten limb immobilised and positioned below heart level.

  4. 04
    Remove constrictive items

    Loosen or remove rings, bracelets, watches and tight clothing around the bite site before swelling makes them impossible to remove.

  5. 05
    Improvise an immobilising splint

    If evacuation is delayed, secure the limb with a firm splint to minimise movement. Do not elevate it, do not use tight bandaging above or below the bite.

  6. 06
    Transfer to hospital without delay

    Transport by ambulance whenever possible. If using a private vehicle, contact the receiving hospital en route so the emergency department and antivenom stock are prepared.

Contraindicated — do not do

These traditional measures are ineffective or harmful and delay definitive care.

  • ✂️

    Incision or suction — do not cut the wound, excise tissue, or attempt to suck out venom. Incision increases bleeding and infection risk.

  • 🩸

    Tight tourniquet — arterial occlusion traps venom in the limb and can cause ischaemic injury and limb loss.

  • 🧊

    Icing or immersion — cold does not neutralise venom and can cause tissue damage.

  • ⚡

    Electric shock or "snakebite gadgets" — no evidence of benefit; they burn tissue and waste critical time.

  • ☕

    Alcohol, caffeine or stimulants — they are not antidotes, mask clinical signs and worsen dehydration.

At hospital

Antivenom for Trinidad's medically significant species is stocked at Port of Spain General Hospital. The decision to administer antivenom is made by medical staff based on clinical signs — not on whether the snake is seen.

Bring the patient to hospital even for suspected "dry" or mild bites. Symptoms of envenoming can be delayed and require observation.
